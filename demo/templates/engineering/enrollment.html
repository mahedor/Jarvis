The recognition stage matches against a gallery of named reference faces. This is where that gallery comes from — a flat bucket of household photos out of Google Photos, turned into named folders of crops, with no face labelled by hand anywhere in the process.

That constraint is not a detail of the implementation. It is the thing that chose the implementation: the algorithm, the shape of the input, and the one failure mode worth being afraid of all follow from refusing to sort crops by hand.

Decisions

01

I would not label any data, and everything else fell out of that

The requirement was never "cluster the faces". It was "do not spend an evening hand-sorting a few hundred crops" — and that one constraint picked the algorithm, the input layout, and the failure mode.

The reference photos came out of Google Photos as one flat bucket, and most of them contain more than one person. So there is no per-face ground truth anywhere in the input: a folder is an event, a photo is a scene, and a face is neither. Nothing in the pile says who anybody is.

The obvious path was to detect every face, dump the crops into a directory and sort them by hand. A few hundred crops, an hour or two, done — and it would have worked. I refused it, because a manual step has to be paid again every time the input changes, and "I'll re-sort the crops" is the kind of chore a personal project quietly dies of.

What runs instead: detect every face in every photo, embed all of them, and run a single HDBSCAN pass over the whole pot. Each person falls out as their own cluster. The only manual step left is renaming the cluster folders it writes — cluster_000/ → michael/ — and deleting the ones that turn out to be strangers. That takes seconds.

collect_faces.py never writes a name. It has no idea who anyone is and cannot acquire one; all it asserts is that a group of crops is the same person. The rename is the labelling step, and it is the whole of it.

"No hand-labelling" is a design constraint, not a preference. Adopt it at the start and it picks your algorithm for you. Adopt it at the end and you have already built the thing that needs labels.

02

The most reliable option was rejected on exactly those grounds

Seed-based selection — one known-good crop per person, everything else matched against it — would have been more robust than clustering. It needs a human to choose that crop, so it was out.

Seeding is the strong answer here and it is worth being honest about that. An anchor crop per identity means the method has no "how many people are in this pile?" to get wrong, the hardest question in the whole problem. It degrades gracefully, and it is trivial to reason about.

But the seed has to be picked, by a person, once per identity — and it has to be a good one, because a bad anchor silently poisons that entire identity. That is the labelling step the requirement forbids, in its smallest and most seductive form. Rejecting the strongest alternative on the constraint is what makes it a constraint rather than a preference.

Two others were weighed and dropped:

density / consensus filter simplest — keep the crops that agree with the bulk of their pile

Needs a cutoff chosen by hand, and assumes each pile has one clear majority identity to agree with. This bucket does not: most photos contain several people, so the "majority" of a pile is not a person.

iterative medoid trimming recompute the centre, drop the furthest, repeat

Couples rejection to aggregation — the thing deciding who belongs is computed from the set it is deciding about — so an early mistake compounds. It is also sensitive to where it starts, which is another way of saying it wants a good seed.

The measure of a constraint is what you turn down that you would otherwise have taken. Seed-based was the better engineering answer to a question I had decided not to ask.

03

HDBSCAN over DBSCAN, for the same reason again

DBSCAN would have handed me an epsilon to tune by hand. HDBSCAN sweeps densities and keeps whichever clusters stay stable, which leaves one knob that is a fact about the household rather than about the embedding space.

DBSCAN needs a neighbourhood radius. There is no principled value for it: the right epsilon depends on the encoder, the crop quality, the lighting and the people. Finding it means running the thing over and over and eyeballing the output — hand-tuning, which is hand-labelling wearing a different hat.

HDBSCAN builds the cluster hierarchy across all densities and extracts the clusters that persist over the widest range of them. There is no radius to choose. What remains is min_cluster_size: the fewest crops that can constitute a person — a question about my household, which I can answer, rather than a question about the geometry of ArcFace's embedding space, which I cannot.

It also has a native notion of noise. The one-off stranger in the background of a single photo is labelled −1 and set aside, instead of being forced into the nearest person's reference set and poisoning it. k-means has nowhere to put that face; it must go in somebody's pile.

Euclidean distance on L2-normalised vectors is monotonic with cosine distance, so clustering with plain euclidean here still clusters by the similarity ArcFace was trained for.

Prefer the algorithm whose leftover parameter is a fact about your problem to the one whose leftover parameter is a fact about your data's geometry. You can answer the first one.

04

The flat bucket is deliberate, and this run proves it

Trusting those names would have merged {{ curation.collisions[0].identities }} different identities into one and misfiled {{ curation.mislabelled_if_voted }} crops — with no error and no warning. {% else %} Trusting the folder a photo sits in would label faces by whose event it was, not by who is in it. {% endif %}

--input-dir is walked recursively and treated as one flat pot. Subfolder names are carried along as metadata — a hint shown in the report to help you name a cluster — and are never used to group anyone. That looks like laziness. It is the opposite.

The tempting alternative is to treat each folder as a label and take a majority vote per pile. It fails in the worst available way: silently, and most convincingly when it is most wrong. Someone who recurs across many of one person's photos forms a clean, high-purity cluster of their own — and gets filed under the folder owner's name. High purity is exactly what you would check to convince yourself it worked.

In a flat bucket a recurring non-household face simply becomes its own cluster, which I look at once and either name or delete. The cost of the flat design is one extra folder to glance at. The cost of the labelled design is three people wrongly enrolled as a fourth.

A label that is usually right is worse than no label at all, because the cases where it is wrong are the ones that look most convincing.

05

min_cluster_size is the only real knob, and it deletes people

HDBSCAN discards any group smaller than min_cluster_size as noise. Set it too high and a real member of the household disappears from the gallery — no error, no warning, no empty folder to notice.

Nothing says anyone is missing.

The exposure is structural rather than bad luck: the people with the fewest photos are the first the knob deletes, and they are also the ones you are least likely to notice the absence of. The thinnest source folder here contributed only {{ curation.folders[-1].photos }} photos, and thinner input survives the quality filter as fewer crops still.

What makes this survivable is that noise is written to disk rather than dropped. All {{ curation.noise }} noise crops go to _noise/, so the check after every run is to open that folder and look for a face that should have been a cluster. A silent failure mode is only tolerable when the artifact makes the silence visible.

When a parameter's failure mode is deletion without an error, the tool has to write down what it deleted. Otherwise the only way to discover the setting was wrong is for the system to fail to greet somebody, months later.

The three surfaced family members are the part worth pointing at, because they are what the flat bucket was for. Nobody asked the tool to look for them; there was no folder for them to be found in. They were in the photos, they recurred often enough to clear min_cluster_size, and so they came out as clusters of their own — which is precisely the behaviour that a folder-derived label would have destroyed.

Clustering is the wrong tool one stage later

It is worth being explicit that this is not how identification works at inference, and that the difference is the point rather than an inconsistency.

at curation — discovery

There are no labels at all, and the actual question is "how many people are in this pot, and which crops belong together?". Nobody knows the answer, including me. That is a discovery problem, and discovery is what clustering is for.

at inference — comparison

The identities are known, the gallery is fixed, and the question is "which of these six, if any, is this face?" — a cosine comparison against stored vectors and a threshold. Clustering here would re-derive, from one frame, something already established from {{ curation.clustered_crops }} crops.

Same embeddings, same distance metric, two different questions. Using the unsupervised method where the labels exist would be as wrong as using the supervised one where they do not.
